Alberta Income Support: Historic Caseload Trends

Analysing 20 years of monthly caseload data to understand how the total caseload has evolved — and what the BFE/ETW breakdown reveals about who is on Income Support and why.

Data: Alberta ALSS Open Data Portal Coverage: April 2005 – September 2025 246 months • 3,722 observations Generated: April 2026

Research Questions

  1. What is the historic trend of Income Support caseload?
  2. How does breaking it down by BFE and ETW change the story?
Research Question 1

The Historic Trend: Twenty Years of Rising Demand

From a low of 24,205 in October 2006 — at the height of the pre-recession Alberta boom — Income Support caseload has grown more than 2.5 times to reach 61,061 in September 2025. The headline trend is one of long-run growth punctuated by three major shocks.

Phase 1 — Boom years (2005–2008): Caseload sat in the mid-20,000s, the lowest in the series. Strong oil prices and tight labour markets pulled potential clients into employment.

Phase 2 — Global Financial Crisis (2008–2011): Caseload surged from ~27,000 to a local peak of ~40,177 (June 2010), a 50% rise in under two years, then partially recovered to the low-to-mid 30,000s.

Phase 3 — Oil price collapse (2015–2019): By far the most sustained increase. Caseload nearly doubled from ~35,000 in early 2015 to 62,021 in January 2020 — the all-time record. The downturn hit ETW workers especially hard (see Section 2).

Phase 4 — COVID-19 disruption (2020–2021): Against all intuition, caseload dropped sharply in 2020 — falling from 62,021 to a trough of ~40,959 by August 2021. Federal pandemic supports (CERB, CRB) temporarily replaced provincial Income Support for many ETW clients.

Phase 5 — Post-pandemic surge (2022–2025): As federal supports ended, caseload rebounded rapidly and is now at 61,061 (September 2025), approaching the 2020 record peak. This recovery is faster and steeper than any previous post-recession rebound.

Research Question 2

The BFE/ETW Breakdown: Two Very Different Stories

⚠ The BFE/ETW breakdown is available from April 2012 onward. The 2008–2012 recession surge therefore pre-dates this split and cannot be attributed directly to either client type.

When caseload is split by client type, the single trend line separates into two distinct trajectories — and the story changes fundamentally.

BFE (Barriers to Full Employment) — clients with significant, often long-term barriers — has been remarkably stable. It moved from ~16,000 in 2012 to a peak of ~23,149 in November 2018, then gradually declined back toward ~18,000–19,000 by 2024–2025. It represents a fairly-fixed structural floor of complex need.

ETW (Expected to Work) — clients who are able-bodied and expected to pursue employment — has been the sole driver of large-scale variation. It has grown from ~18,500 in April 2012 to over 42,800 in mid-2025, a 131% increase. Every major surge in total caseload since 2012 is primarily an ETW surge.

The mix has also shifted dramatically: BFE was roughly equal to ETW in 2012–2013 (~50/50). By September 2025, ETW accounts for ~69% of the caseload and BFE only ~31%. The caseload is increasingly composed of people the system expects to work — but who cannot find or hold employment.

What the BFE/ETW split reveals

The total trend line obscures a fundamental division. BFE growth is modest and likely reflects demographic trends and policy thresholds. The growth story is entirely about ETW — people whom the system classifies as work-ready but who remain on Income Support.

Within ETW, the largest and fastest-growing subcategory is Not Working – Available for Work: clients who are available for employment but not finding it. This group grew from ~7,200 in 2012 to over 27,000 in 2025, a near four-fold increase — and is the single component most sensitive to labour market conditions.

This matters for policy interpretation: a caseload that is 69% ETW is not primarily a story about chronic need or disability — it is substantially a story about labour market accessibility. When the labour market tightens (2006–2008, briefly in 2021), ETW numbers fall quickly. When it softens, they rise dramatically.

Deeper Look

Inside ETW: Available-for-Work Clients Are the Main Driver

ETW clients divide into three subgroups: those currently working (earning-supplemented), those available for work but not working, and those temporarily unavailable (e.g., short-term illness, caretaking).

The available-for-work group utterly dominates the ETW trend. It has risen from ~7,200 to ~27,000 — accounting for the vast majority of the overall 20-year caseload growth. The working group has remained small and stable (2,000–3,500), and the temporarily unavailable group has grown moderately from ~8,500 to ~13,000.

The COVID period produced a striking pattern: all three ETW groups dropped sharply in 2020 as CERB replaced Income Support, but the available-for-work group has since more than fully recovered — reaching record levels not seen even in 2019. This suggests persistent labour market exclusion for this population beyond what can be explained by the 2020 shock alone.
